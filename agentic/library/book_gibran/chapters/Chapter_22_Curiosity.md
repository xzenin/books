# Chapter 22: Curiosity

And a scholar who had closed his books said, "I have learned all that can be known, and the world has become small and without mystery. Speak to us of Curiosity, for mine has turned to ash."

And he answered, saying:

To stop questioning is to stop breathing the air of the spirit.

Curiosity is the holy itch of the mind, the refusal to see the horizon as a wall. It is the child within you who keeps the world from growing old, who wakes each morning and asks, "What wonders have I not yet seen?"

You say the world has become small? Nay, it is your vision that has shrunk.

For the man who believes he knows all things has built a prison of his own certainty, and he sits within it, congratulating himself on the strength of the bars.

But I say to you: The wise man is not the one who has filled his vessel to the brim. The wise man is the one who knows his vessel has no bottom, and he drinks from the river of mystery until his final breath.

A single blade of grass contains more riddles than all the libraries of the learned.

Can you explain the hunger of its root? Can you name the voice that calls the water upward through its veins? Can you tell me why it bends toward the light, though it has no eyes to see?

You cannot. And in that not-knowing lies the doorway to the Infinite.

Curiosity is not the enemy of peace; it is the companion of wonder. The questioning mind does not overthrow the throne of truth—it polishes the throne until the light reflected from it blinds the proud.

Verily, the child asks, "Why?" a hundred times, and the world calls this foolishness. But the child is wiser than the elder who has ceased to ask, for the child knows that every answer is a seed that grows into ten new questions.

You who have closed your books, thinking yourself complete, are like a man who has eaten one fruit and declared, "I have tasted all the orchards of the earth."

There is no end to the knowing, because the universe is not a scroll that can be read and set aside. It is a living thing, and it grows even as you seek to understand it.

The stars do not stand still so that you may count them. They move, they burn, they are born and they die, and each one carries a story that no tongue has yet told.

And the heart of another person—have you read that book? Have you learned all the chambers of the human soul, all the corners where fear hides and love waits in ambush?

You have not. You cannot. For the soul is deeper than the ocean, and even those who dive into it for a lifetime return to the surface gasping and saying, "There is more. There is always more."

Curiosity is the lamp you carry into the dark, not to conquer the darkness, but to walk beside it with humility.

It is the whisper that says, "I do not know, but I will seek. I have not seen, but I will look. I have not heard, but I will listen."

You ask, "What shall I do to keep the flame of curiosity alive?"

And I say to you: Go to the child who plays in the dust and watch how she turns a stone into a kingdom. Go to the fool who asks, "Why do birds sing?" and do not mock him, but sit with him and wonder.

Read not only the books of men, but the book of the earth—the language of the wind in the reeds, the sermon of the river over stones, the poetry of the fire that consumes and gives warmth in the same breath.

And when you believe you have learned a thing, hold it lightly, as you would hold a sparrow. For if you grip it too tightly, you will crush it, and if you open your hand, it may fly away and teach you that you never possessed it at all.

The question is the root; the answer is the fruit. But if you eat only the fruit and burn the root, you will starve in a season.

Let your mind remain hungry. Let your heart remain open. Let your spirit refuse to believe that the horizon is the end of the world.

For the world has no end, and neither does the soul that seeks to know it.

When the scholar's world became small, the prophet led him to a single blade of grass and asked, "Can you explain the hunger of this root?"

And the scholar's world became vast once more, and he wept for the years he had spent sleeping in the prison of his pride.
