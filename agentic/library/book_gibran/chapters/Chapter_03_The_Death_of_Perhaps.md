# Chapter 3: The Death of 'Perhaps'

And two men stood on opposite sides of a river, each claiming the water flowed only for his own village, and they said, "Speak to us of Certainty."

And he answered, saying:

You have made gods of your opinions and built temples to your own righteousness. You stand on opposite banks and curse one another for drinking from the same stream, never understanding that the river knows no master.

This is the death of 'perhaps,' and with it dies the possibility of wisdom.

The man who says, "I know, and there is no other knowing," has closed the door of his soul. He has made of his mind a tomb, and he buries truth there before it has a chance to breathe. He is like the tree that refuses to bend in the storm, and so the storm breaks him.

But the one who says, "Perhaps I am wrong, perhaps there is more," this one is a vessel that the Infinite can fill. This one is a door through which light may enter.

You ask, "But must I doubt everything? Must I stand forever in the fog of uncertainty?"

And I say to you: Uncertainty is not fog. Uncertainty is the fertile soil. Certainty is the stone upon which nothing grows.

The farmer who plants his seed does not know if the rain will come, yet he plants. The mother who bears her child does not know what manner of soul she has brought into the world, yet she loves. To live is to walk the middle path, where faith and doubt hold hands like two old friends.

Verily, the beauty of the word 'perhaps' is that it leaves room for the stranger's truth. It is the bridge between your village and mine. When you destroy this bridge, you doom yourself to an island of your own making, surrounded by waters you refuse to cross.

I have watched you build walls of certainty, brick by brick, until you can no longer see the sky. You say, "I am safe here, within my walls." But safety is not the same as life. The bird in the cage is safe from the hawk, but it has forgotten how to sing.

And what is this intolerance you wear like armor? It is fear dressed in the robes of strength. It is the child who covers his ears and shouts, "I cannot hear you!" believing this makes him wise.

The true sage walks among many truths and collects them like shells on the shore. He does not say, "This shell is the only shell." He says, "Behold the many shapes that the sea creates, and in their variety, I see the hand of the Divine."

When you meet the stranger whose words trouble you, do not reach for your stones. Reach instead for your silence. Listen as the earth listens to the rain—without judgment, without resistance. Let his truth fall upon you, and if it is false, it will wash away. But if it carries even a seed of wisdom, that seed will take root, and you will grow taller than you were before.

A child walked into the water and became wet from both sides, laughing because the river had no name for either man. The child knew what the men had forgotten: that the river does not belong to the one who shouts the loudest, but to the one who drinks with humility.

Release your grip on certainty. Let your hands be open. For the man with clenched fists can receive nothing, but the man with open palms may catch the falling rain, and in that rain is the answer to every question you have been too proud to ask.
