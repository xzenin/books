# Chapter 7: Ambition

And a young man who stood at the foot of the mountain said, "Speak to us of the Hunger to Rise."

And he answered, saying:

A young man once climbed the highest peak to touch the moon, leaving his sandals and his songs at the base of the mountain. He climbed through day and night, through heat and cold, his eyes fixed always on the summit, never looking back at what he left behind.

This is ambition: the divine hunger that lifts you toward the stars. But listen closely, for it is also the hunger that can leave you starving.

Ambition is the wind in your sails. It is the fire that wakes you before dawn. It is the voice that says, "You are more than this moment; you are becoming." Without it, you would drift like a leaf on water, going wherever the current takes you, never choosing your own shore.

But ambition, like fire, must be tended carefully. Feed it too much and it will consume your house. Feed it too little and it will die, leaving you cold in the darkness.

You ask, "How high should I climb? When do I stop?"

And I say to you: The question is not how high, but what you leave behind in the climbing, and whether you can hear the songs you abandoned calling you home.

The man who climbs for glory alone will reach the top and find it empty. He will stand on the summit, the wind howling around him, and discover that the moon is still as far away as it was when he stood at the base. And when he looks down, he will see that the valley where his heart once lived is now too distant to return to.

But the man who climbs with purpose—not to prove himself to others, but to see what he is capable of becoming—this man carries the valley with him. He does not abandon his songs; he learns new ones. And when he reaches the peak, he does not claim it as his throne. He simply looks out, understands what he came to understand, and then descends to share the view with those still climbing.

Ambition asks you to sacrifice, and this is right. The seed must break open to become the tree. The caterpillar must dissolve to become the butterfly. But there is a difference between sacrifice and self-destruction.

Sacrifice is giving up the small for the large—the comfort of the moment for the achievement of a lifetime. Self-destruction is giving up everything, including the reason you began climbing in the first place.

Do not sacrifice your children on the altar of your ambition. Do not trade the love of those who know your name for the applause of those who will forget you. Do not become so enamored with the peak that you forget the valley is where life is lived.

Verily, ambition is a ladder to heaven, but if you climb so high that you cannot hear the voices of your beloved calling you to dinner, you have climbed too far.

The mountain will always be there. It does not move. But your children grow, your parents age, your friends fade away. These are the things that move, and if you are not careful, they will move past you while your eyes are fixed on the summit.

At the top of the mountain, he found the moon was still far away, but the view of his own valley showed him where his heart truly lived. He saw the fires of the evening hearth. He heard, faint as a whisper, the laughter of his beloved. And he understood that the climb was not meant to take him away from life, but to teach him what life was worth returning to.

Climb, yes. Reach, yes. But do not mistake the peak for the purpose. The purpose is not to arrive but to become worthy of the arriving. And when you have seen what you came to see, descend with humility, carrying the wisdom of the height back to the valley where it can be planted and grow.

For the valley needs the mountain's vision, and the mountain needs the valley's roots. One without the other is incomplete.

Let your ambition be a tool in your hand, not a fire that consumes you. Use it to build, not to burn. And when you reach the place you have been striving for, remember to turn around and extend your hand to the one still climbing behind you.

For the true measure of your ascent is not how high you climbed, but how many you lifted with you.
