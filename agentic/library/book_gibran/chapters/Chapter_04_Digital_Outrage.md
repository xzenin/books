# Chapter 4: Digital Outrage

And a woman who carried the weight of many grievances said, "Speak to us of Wrath."

And he answered, saying:

You have learned to send your anger on wings of lightning, and it arrives at the doorstep of your enemy before your heart has time to cool. You whisper a grievance into the air, and the air becomes a storm of stones that flies across the world in the blinking of an eye.

This is the curse of speed: It has stolen from you the gift of distance.

In the days of your fathers, when a man was wronged, he would walk to his neighbor's house to deliver his complaint. And in the walking, the wind would touch his face. The sun would remind him of other days. Perhaps he would see a child playing, or a bird building its nest, and these small things would temper the fire in his chest.

By the time he reached the door, his anger had been softened by the journey. He knocked with a hand that still held heat, but no longer held murder. And often, the two men would talk and find understanding, for distance had given both of them time to remember their shared humanity.

But you, you strike from your chamber with words that travel faster than thought. You wound without walking. You condemn without seeing the face of the condemned. And when the lightning you threw completes its circle, you discover you have wounded your own brother, for the world is smaller than your rage knew.

The words you send into the invisible web do not dissolve like breath in the winter air. They remain, carved into a surface harder than stone, permanent as scars. The man you judge today will carry your judgment for all his days, and his children's children will read your wrath long after you have forgotten why you were angry.

Is this the legacy you wish to leave? A trail of wounds that never heal?

You say, "But he wronged me! He spoke falsely! He deserves my fury!"

And I say to you: Even if he deserves your fury, does the world deserve the poison that your fury creates? For wrath is not a sword that cuts only the guilty. It is a flood that drowns the innocent fields along with the wicked.

Before you release your lightning, ask yourself: Have I walked the distance? Have I let the wind cool my face? Have I imagined standing before this person, seeing the fear in their eyes, the trembling of their hands?

Or have I become the faceless accuser, the voice without a body, the stone-thrower who hides in the crowd?

Verily, it is easier to destroy than to build. It takes one moment to burn a forest, but a lifetime for the trees to grow back. You believe your outrage is righteousness, but righteousness that cannot wait for the morning sun is not righteousness—it is vengeance wearing a holy mask.

There is a time for anger. When the powerful crush the weak, when cruelty parades as law, when silence would make you complicit in evil—yes, then speak. But speak with the weight of consideration, not the speed of impulse. Speak as one who knows that words, once released, become birds that you cannot call back to your hand.

When the storm clears, you will find that the lightning you threw has traveled in a circle. It has struck your own home, scorched your own garden, frightened your own children. For in this age of instant malice, there is no safe distance between the accuser and the accused. We are all standing in the same storm, and every bolt we throw illuminates our own faces.

Walk before you speak. Wait before you condemn. Let the fire in your chest become embers, and from those embers, speak words that warm rather than words that burn.

For the world has enough ashes. What it needs now is light.
