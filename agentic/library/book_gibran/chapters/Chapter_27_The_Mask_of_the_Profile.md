# Chapter 27: The Mask of the Profile

And a young woman who carried a tablet of glowing glass said, "Speak to us of the Mask of the Profile, for I spend my days carving an image of myself to show the world, and I no longer know which is real—the mask or the face beneath it."

And he answered, saying:

You have become the sculptor of shadows.

You carve images of light and glass to show the world, painting yourself not as you are, but as you wish to be seen. You remove the wrinkles of your sorrow, the scars of your struggle, the weariness of your journey.

And in time, you begin to love the image more than the soul behind it.

This is the great seduction of the mirrors that speak with a thousand tongues: they offer you the power to be perfect, to be loved by strangers, to be admired without ever being known.

But I say to you: To be admired is not the same as to be loved.

The image you create cannot feel the rain. It cannot taste the bread of sorrow or drink the wine of joy. It cannot weep when your heart is broken or laugh until your belly aches.

It is a ghost, and you have given it more care than you have given your own living flesh.

Verily, there is a girl who spends all her days painting a portrait of herself that is beautiful and without flaw, while her own face grows pale and weary from neglect.

She shows the portrait to the world and the world says, "How beautiful you are!" and she smiles, but the smile does not reach her heart.

For deep within, she knows the truth: they do not love her. They love the image. And if the image were to crack, if the glass were to shatter, they would turn away and seek another portrait to admire.

You say, "But this is how we speak to one another now. This is the language of our age."

And I say to you: Then learn a new language, or become mute in the ways that matter.

For the tongue of the image is a tongue that cannot speak truth. It can only speak illusion.

It can say, "I am happy," even when you are drowning in sorrow. It can say, "I am strong," even when you are breaking. It can say, "I am loved," even when you are achingly alone.

And the world will believe the illusion, and you will be surrounded by voices that praise the ghost while the living soul within you starves for recognition.

You ask, "How then shall I present myself to the world? Must I show every wound, every failure, every moment of weakness?"

And I say to you: No. You need not perform your pain for the entertainment of strangers.

But neither should you perform a perfection that does not exist.

There is a middle path, and it is called authenticity.

It is the courage to say, "I am neither perfect nor broken. I am human, and I am walking the road as best I can."

It is the willingness to let others see the lines on your face that were carved by laughter and tears. It is the freedom to say, "I do not know," or "I am struggling," or "I need help."

For in that vulnerability lies the doorway to true connection.

The mask of the profile keeps others at a distance. It says, "You may admire me, but you may not know me."

But the human heart hungers not for admiration, but for recognition.

It hungers to be seen—truly seen—and still be found worthy of love.

Verily, the greatest loneliness is not the absence of people. The greatest loneliness is being surrounded by people who know only your mask.

You stand in a crowded room, and you smile, and you speak, and you perform the dance of the image. And when you return home, you remove the mask, and you sit in the silence, and you realize: no one knows you.

No one knows the doubts that keep you awake at night. No one knows the dreams you are afraid to speak aloud. No one knows the secret sorrow you carry in your chest like a stone.

And you are lonely in a way that cannot be soothed by the voices of a thousand admirers.

For they admire the ghost, not you.

Break the glass. Let your weariness be seen.

Let the world know that you are not a perfect image carved in crystal, but a living, breathing soul who stumbles and falls and rises again.

Let them see the cracks, for the cracks are where the light gets in.

And those who turn away when they see the cracks were never meant to walk with you. They were tourists in the gallery of your image, and they will move on to the next exhibit.

But those who stay—those who say, "I see your cracks, and I do not love you less for them"—they are the ones who will know you.

And to be known is the greatest gift one soul can give to another.

You say, "But if I show myself as I truly am, I will not be loved."

And I say to you: If you show yourself as you truly are, you will not be loved by everyone. But you will be loved by the right ones.

And the love of the right ones is worth more than the admiration of a thousand strangers.

For the stranger loves the mask because it asks nothing of them. But the one who loves you—truly loves you—loves the soul beneath the mask, and that love is a fire that does not go out when the wind blows.

The portrait cannot feel the rain. It cannot know the warmth of a hand that holds yours in the dark. It cannot hear the voice that says, "You are enough, just as you are."

But you can.

You, the living soul, can know these things.

So put down the tablet of glowing glass. Step away from the mirror that reflects only what you wish to be seen.

And walk into the world as you are—flawed, weary, scarred by the journey, but alive.

For only the living can be loved, and only the living can love in return.
